## 3. Trend / Compensation Line

* A **compensation line** is another term for a **trend line** or **line of best fit**.
* It compensates for deviations of data points and visualizes the average relationship between variables.
* Example: In a scatterplot of sales versus advertising spend, it shows how sales generally increase as advertising increases.
<br/>
### Adding a Linear Trend Line

```r
geom_smooth(method = "lm", se = FALSE)
```

* `lm`: linear model
* `se`: standard error (confidence band)

## 6. Data Distribution & Visualization

* **Always consider the distribution of your data when comparing groups.**
* Always **show the distribution** to your audience, especially for non-experts.

### Showing Uncertainty: Error Bars

* Error bars visually represent **variability or uncertainty** in the data.
* They indicate how precise a measurement is or how far the true value might be from the estimate.
* Error bars always represent a **range** that includes a percentage of values.

---

## 7. Types of Error Bars

### 1. Standard Deviation (SD)

* Represents a percentage of the data.
* Answers the question:
*Where will the next data point likely be?*
* Similar interpretation to a boxplot.

### 2. Standard Error / Confidence Interval (SE / CI)

* Represents a percentage of possible population mean values.
* Answers the question:
*If we took another sample, where would the mean likely be?*
* Used to build **confidence intervals**.

> Error bars assume an underlying distribution, usually a **normal distribution** or **Student’s t distribution**.

---

## 8. Normal Distribution

* After sorting the data, the most frequent or densest values are located around the center (mean).
* Also called the **bell curve**.

### Empirical Rule (68–95–99.7 Rule)

* ~68% of values lie within **±1 SD** of the mean
* ~95% of values lie within **±2 SD** of the mean
* ~99.7% of values lie within **±3 SD** of the mean

---

## 9. Standard Deviation (SD)

+ **Not a statistical test**.
+ Measures **variability** in the data.
+ Indicates:
    + How spread out data points are around the mean.
    + How reliable the mean is as a representative value.
    + Large SD ≠ invalid data (e.g., biological data is often highly variable).

---

## 10. Standard Error (SE / SEM)

+ Indicates how close the sample mean is to the true population mean.
+ Calculated as:

```jupyter
SEM = σ / √n
```
Where:
+ <span>`σ` = standard deviation</span>
+ <span>`n` = number of observations</span>
+ <span>Larger sample size -> smaller standard error.</span>

+ <span>Example: If `SE = 2`, the sample mean may differ from the true mean by ~2 units.</span>

---

## 11. Confidence Interval (CI)

+ A **range of values** that likely contains the true population parameter.
+ Calculated as:

```
    estimate ± variation
```

+ Interpreted as:
*If we repeated the experiment many times, the true value would fall within this range a certain percentage of the time.*

---

## 12. One-Sample t-Test

```r
t.test(dataset3$age, mu = 35)
```

* Tests whether the sample mean differs significantly from a given value (`mu`).

### Hypotheses

* **Null Hypothesis (H₀):** mean(age) = 35
* **Alternative Hypothesis (H₁):** mean(age) ≠ 35

## 13. Linear Models in R (`lm()`)

* Use `+` to model **independent effects**:

```r
    y ~ x1 + x2
```

* Use `*` to include **interaction effects**:

```r
    y ~ x1 * x2
```

Explanation:

* `+`: effects of variables separately
* `*`: separate effects **and** their combined interaction effect
